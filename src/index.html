 THE CAPTAIN’S FIELD GUIDE

Be hard to find.

01 / Listen

Passive sonar gives you a bearing, not a location. The chart draws an uncertain line. An active ping estimates range and can alert nearby hunters. Dashed spokes show direction only. A LAUNCH or SPLASH spoke starts where the crew heard the sound. Diamonds mark estimated positions, with the outer ring at the selected range. Peaks on the hydrophone trace show bearing around the sub, from bow through starboard, stern and port, back to bow. Scribbler prints the bottom in strips. Painter sweeps ahead. Select Start scan to transmit. Survey draws a quieter, coarser image. Detail is quicker and easier to hear. A friendly signal lamp can report a recent sub contact when you surface.

02 / Take a look

At 15 meters or less, raise the scope. Drag to turn, scroll to zoom. Looking identifies ships and estimates range. Take actual camera exposures with F; a merchant silhouette within five degrees of the reticle also counts toward reconnaissance. Open Photos to inspect archived images. The scope shows BOW, VIEW and the relative angle. CENTER ON BOW looks ahead. TURN SUB TO VIEW orders the current sight bearing while the scope holds that direction as the hull catches up. Normal scope angle is relative to the hull. Fishing boats are neutral. A scope and a surfaced hull can be spotted.

03 / Stay alive

Electric draws from the battery. Diesel needs air at 14 meters or shallower. Choose Charge to replenish the battery, Drive to send generator power to the motor, or Battery assist to spend stored power when you order more thrust than the generator can supply. R limits speed to 3 knots. Deep water muffles you. Impacts lower your depth limit. Check Sub, listen for the pressure alarm and rise if it sounds. Damage Control seals leaks and pumps the bilge.

04 / Leave quietly

Every patrol carries torpedoes. Select a contact in Contacts, rise above 100 m, then launch. After its arming run it uses its own active sonar to steer toward acoustic returns. It prefers the selected contact but may acquire another loud vessel. Shallow-running torpedoes leave visible wakes. Escorts hunt noise and drop charges. Turn, dive, release a noisemaker. Damage control slowly patches the hull and flooding at a battery cost.

05 / Borrow another pair of eyes

Deploy your inspection drone at 280 meters or less, while the sub is at 3 knots or less. Keep within the 100-meter control cable in three dimensions; a parted cable loses the ROV for the patrol. By default W pitches the nose up, S pitches down, and A/D turn left/right. The up/down arrows adjust thrust. Space orders all stop. On a phone, swipe the camera view to aim, use the throttle slider for ahead or astern, and tap All Stop to hover. Settings can reverse vertical steering for both keyboard and swipes. The helm shows actual and requested values; the vehicle responds gradually. A 10% alarm warns of low drone battery. Return before it runs out. Docking charges the drone and repairs its hull, camera, thrusters and buoyancy. Electric charging uses the sub’s battery; diesel supplies it directly. Your sub keeps its orders while you’re away.

06 / Know your way home

Choose 5× or 20× for travel. New contacts and attacks return you to 1×. Drag the chart to pan and use the wheel to zoom. Click to plot a waypoint; requested depth stays yours to manage. After your task, return to the green extraction circle and surface for pickup. While paused, helm and mission actions are disabled. Esc pauses; progress autosaves locally.

Read the water beneath you

Helm depth is measured from mean sea level. Water depth runs from the surface to the seabed. Below Keel is the space under your hull, accounting for its five-meter radius. The drone station shows clearance below the drone. Shallow clearance is highlighted.

Use the weather

The bridge watch shows the date and UTC time. Each twelve-hour watch may bring passing submarines, ships or convoys. A captain may turn away, challenge you or open fire. A short-running torpedo can draw a response. Choose a starting time and weather before a new patrol. Night and storms shorten visual detection range; running lights can still betray ships beyond silhouette range. Rough seas can wash over your lens. A full environmental day takes 48 simulated minutes. Changing fronts develop gradually.

Listen for the flash

Lightning has a location. Thunder follows after sound travels to your moving sub. Submerged thunder is quieter and muffled further with depth. Pausing freezes sound arrivals; time compression speeds their travel through game time.

Hear traffic and trouble

Enable Sound to hear nearby engines grow louder as you close and pan as you turn. Depth charges sink to their fuse depth, detonate, and send out a pressure front. Nearby explosions produce louder, sharper shocks; distant ones rumble. Changing course and depth before detonation can save you.

Rescue and survey

Plot the marked mission site with PLOT APPROACH. The sub stops beside it at the requested rescue depth. Undersea rescue needs 30–75 m separation, the stated depth ±5 m and ≤0.6 kn. Surface rescue needs 30–85 m and depth under 1 m. Hold for assessment, survivor transfers and the team’s return. Survivor counts start unknown and can fall with time. Survey at 0.5–3 kn within 100 m and 32 m of wreck depth for 45 seconds, or take three drone photographs from sides at least 55° apart.

A submarine with consequences

Ballast changes buoyancy even at all stop. Moving dive planes add lift. Floodwater weighs the sub down; pumps and repair crew must keep up. Open Subsystem Condition to inspect damage. A lost periscope stays lost, but the conning tower gives a wide view when fully surfaced. Drag vertically there to look down at your deck. The drone and wrecks have solid hulls. Shallow, fast propellers cavitate, making visible vapor and more noise.

Guns and aircraft

The deck gun only works fully surfaced. Open Gun, drag horizontally/vertically to aim, and hold FIRE or J. Select a visible target and use AIM for an estimated lead. Repeated waterline hits flood and disable ships; they can sink later. DU explosive rounds are a fictional exception to the 1972 equipment. Ships can radio the island base after detection. Aircraft prepare, take off and fly here before attacking. Dive to deny a visual strafing target; bombs can still fall over your last reported location.

Repeatable, different patrols

Each new patrol varies traffic, routes, mission location and objectives. Leave the patrol seed empty for fresh orders, or enter a number to replay the same setup. Tiebreaker carries three torpedoes. Other patrols carry six. On touch screens, the fixed helm shows actual and requested values. Hold Port/Starboard or Rise/Dive; move the vertical speed slider for ahead/astern. Tap a readout for exact orders. Mission, Contacts and Sub open compact sheets. Use Active Ping in Sonar, +/− for chart or optical zoom, and ALIGN for scope orders. The ROV strip shows its distance even while you pilot the sub. Slow to three knots for pickup; a flashing warning means the ROV cannot catch you.

Classified orbital reconnaissance

On Chart, choose Recon, then tap the center of a 10 km square and request a pass. A deep request queues until you rise to 15 m for uplink. Watch tasking, exposures, processing and reception progress. Surface reception is quick; shallow submerged reception is slow and holds below 30 m. After delivery, wait eight minutes before another request. Storms block imaging; clouds give partial estimates. Night shows running lights without new identities. The electronic imaging/relay prototype is a deliberate fictional exception to 1972.

Keep the evidence

Photos stores actual scope and ROV exposures plus monochrome recon plates with capture and receipt times. The chart estimates positions from those older observations; uncertainty grows during transmission and afterward. A1, A2 and later contact codes remain attached after identification. Satellite images show surface ships only. Camera photographs can be taken outside mission objectives, but mission credit still requires the briefed subject and framing.
